Drive around Knox County and you'll spot them: retaining walls leaning forward, bulging in the
middle, or already blown out. A retaining wall does serious structural work — it holds back tons of
soil and water on our hilly, clay-heavy lots. When one fails, it's rarely bad luck. It's almost
always one of these.

## 1. No drainage behind the wall

This is the number-one cause. Soil behind a wall soaks up rain and holds it (especially our clay).
That water adds enormous **hydrostatic pressure** against the back of the wall. Without a way to
relieve it — gravel backfill and a perforated drain pipe — that pressure pushes the wall forward
until it leans or collapses.

**The fix:** clean, free-draining gravel behind the wall and a drain line that carries water away.

## 2. Backfilled with the wrong material

Many failed walls were backfilled with the same heavy clay they're holding back. Clay holds water and
swells. A proper wall is backfilled with free-draining stone, not the excavated dirt.

## 3. A weak or missing base

A retaining wall is only as good as the footing under it. A compacted, level base of the right depth
spreads the load. Set the first course on soft or uneven ground and the whole wall follows it down.

## 4. No soil reinforcement on taller walls

Taller walls (generally over about 3–4 feet) need **geogrid** — layers of reinforcement that extend
back into the soil and tie the whole mass together. Build a tall wall without it and gravity slowly
wins.

## 5. No batter (set-back)

Good segmental walls lean back slightly into the hill (called batter) so gravity works *with* them.
A dead-vertical wall has nothing helping it resist the soil pressure.

## 6. Skipping the permit and engineering

In Knox County and surrounding areas, walls over a certain height (commonly 4 feet from the bottom of
the footing) typically require an **engineered design and a permit.** That's not red tape — it's the
math that keeps a big wall standing. Skipping it is a gamble with tons of soil.

## How we build walls that hold

Every retaining wall we build starts underground:

- A compacted base and proper footing
- Free-draining gravel backfill — never the excavated clay
- A drain line to relieve water pressure
- Geogrid soil reinforcement on taller walls
- Proper batter and compaction lift by lift
- Engineered design and permits when the height calls for it

The finish — block, natural stone or boulders — is the easy part. The reason our walls are still
standing straight in ten years is everything behind them.
