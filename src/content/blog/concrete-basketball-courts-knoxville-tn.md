## Why Concrete Is the Right Base for a Basketball Court

You have three realistic options for a home court surface: asphalt, modular plastic tile over a slab, or concrete.

Asphalt is the cheapest up front and the most expensive over time. It softens in a Tennessee July, it oxidizes, it needs resealing, and it never gives you a true, consistent bounce. Modular tiles are a great product — but they still need a slab underneath them. You're not choosing tiles *instead of* concrete. You're choosing tiles *on top of* concrete.

That leaves concrete as the foundation of every good court, whether you finish it with a broom texture, an acrylic sport coating, or snap-together tile.

Concrete gives you:

- **A true, predictable ball bounce.** Flat, hard, and consistent — the same response a gym floor gives, which is what actually makes practice transfer to game day.
- **A 30-plus year surface.** With proper base prep and thickness, a court poured today is still a court when your kids bring their own kids over.
- **A surface that takes anything.** Basketball today, pickleball next spring, roller hockey, scooters, chalk, a graduation party with 40 people and a couple of folding tables.
- **Low maintenance.** Blow it off. Hose it down. That's the routine.

## The Real Benefit: Reps, Confidence, and Time Off the Screen

We build these for homeowners, but the people who use them are almost always kids — and the parents who've had one for a season or two tell us the same handful of things.

## Sizing: What Actually Fits on a Knoxville Lot

You do not need a regulation court, and most people shouldn't build one. Here's how the sizes break down:

| Court type | Dimensions | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| Shooting strip / key | 20' x 30' | Small yards, driveway extensions, form work and free throws |
| Half court (compact) | 30' x 30' | The most common backyard court we pour |
| Half court (standard) | 42' x 50' | Real half-court games, 3-on-3 |
| Three-quarter court | 42' x 74' | Larger acreage properties |
| Full court (high school) | 50' x 84' | Rare residential; needs a flat, open acre-plus |

For reference, a high school court is 50' x 84' and an NBA court is 50' x 94'. Most families are happiest with a **30' x 50' or 42' x 50'** — big enough for a real game, small enough to fit an average East Tennessee lot without eating the whole yard.

**Add 3 to 5 feet of run-out space on every side** past the playing surface. Kids chase loose balls, and that extra concrete is cheap compared to a shin on a retaining wall.

On sloped West Knoxville and Farragut lots this is often the cheapest place to put a court because the grade is already established.

## What East Tennessee Ground Does to a Court (and How We Handle It)

This is the part that separates a court that plays flat in ten years from one that doesn't.

**Our soil moves.** Much of Knox, Blount, and Loudon County sits on clay over limestone. Clay swells when it's wet and shrinks when it's dry, and we get roughly 48 inches of rain a year to feed that cycle. A slab poured on undisturbed, unprepped clay will telegraph every bit of that movement.

**So the base is where the money goes.** We excavate, undercut soft spots, and build a compacted crushed stone base — compacted in lifts, not dumped and raked. That base is doing two jobs: giving the slab a uniform bearing surface, and giving water somewhere to go.

**Drainage is designed before the pour, not after.** A court is a big impervious surface. Water that used to soak into your yard now has to go somewhere, and if that somewhere is your foundation or your neighbor's yard, you've traded one problem for a worse one. We slope courts at about 1% — roughly an inch every 8 to 10 feet, in one direction — which sheds water without affecting play. Where the site needs it, we'll tie in French drains, channel drains, or a swale to carry it away.

**Thickness and reinforcement.** We pour courts at 4 inches as our standard — heavier than a typical walkway — with rebar or mesh set on chairs, not laid on the ground and pulled up by hand mid-pour. On properties where the court doubles as a parking pad or a turnaround for heavier vehicles, we'll step up to 5 inches. That's a design conversation, and it does change the price.

### About Control Joints — Let's Be Straight With You

Concrete shrinks as it cures. That's chemistry, not craftsmanship, and it's true of every slab ever poured, by anyone, anywhere. The job of a good contractor isn't to promise it won't happen — it's to **control where it goes.**

That's what control joints are. We cut them in a planned grid, at the right depth (a quarter of the slab thickness), at the right time, so the slab relieves stress in straight lines we chose instead of random ones it chose. On a court we lay that grid out to align with the striping so the joints read as part of the design rather than an interruption.

Anybody who tells you their concrete won't ever crack is either new or selling you something. What you want is somebody who plans the joints, preps the base right, gets the thickness and reinforcement correct, and manages the cure. That's the whole game.

## Finish Options

**Broom finish (standard).** Light broom texture, sealed. Best traction in the wet, lowest cost, most forgiving over time. This is what most of our customers choose.

**Acrylic sport coating.** The colored, cushioned system you see on tennis and pickleball courts — usually two tones with the key and three-point arc in a contrasting color. Looks fantastic, plays true, and needs recoating every 5 to 8 years. Has to go over fully cured concrete, so it's a second visit about 30 days after the pour.

**Modular tile.** Snap-together polypropylene tiles over the slab. Slightly softer on knees and ankles, drains instantly, and individual tiles can be swapped if one gets damaged. Highest cost of the three.

**Striping.** Painted lines over sealed concrete, in whatever configuration you want — basketball only, or basketball plus pickleball. And since we're in East Tennessee, yes, we've painted a fair number of orange-and-white checkerboard baselines. That's a conversation to have during the design visit.

## Extras Worth Planning For *Before* the Pour

Some of these are difficult or impossible to add later. Decide now:

- **In-ground goal sleeve.** Set during the pour. An adjustable in-ground system with a 60" or 72" tempered glass backboard is night-and-day better than a portable base, and it doesn't scoot when somebody hangs on the rim. If your kid is going to grow into a 10-foot rim, get adjustable.
- **Conduit for lighting.** Run it under the slab now. Four pole-mounted LED fixtures turn a court from a 6-month asset into a 12-month one, and in the winter that's the difference between practicing and not.
- **A second goal.** Even on a half court, opposing goals let you run full-court drills lengthwise.
- **Fencing or a rebound net.** On a sloped lot or near a road, a 10-foot net behind the goal saves a lot of walking.
- **Seating and shade.** A small [paver patio](/pavers/) or seat wall at the sideline gives parents somewhere to sit. We build a lot of courts as part of a [larger outdoor living plan](/outdoor-living/) — court, patio, fire feature, and grill station on one job.

## What Does a Concrete Basketball Court Cost in Knoxville?

Honest answer: it depends on the size, the grade, and the finish — and any contractor who gives you a firm number over the phone without seeing your lot is guessing.

That said, here's what **the concrete floor itself** runs on a typical flat, accessible lot, so you can tell whether this project is in your neighborhood. These figures cover excavation, compacted stone base, reinforcement, a 4" pour, finishing, and cut control joints:

| Court size | Square feet | Concrete cost |
|---|---|---|
| 20' x 30' shooting strip | 600 sq ft | $6,600 – $7,800 |
| 30' x 50' half court | 1,500 sq ft | $16,500 – $19,500 |
| 42' x 50' half court | 2,100 sq ft | $23,100 – $27,300 |

**That is the slab and nothing else.** We'd rather show you a clean number for the part we control than bury a bunch of allowances in one figure and surprise you later. Everything below is quoted separately on your proposal:

- **Striping and painted lines** — basketball, pickleball, or both
- **Acrylic sport coating** — court surfacing is a specialty trade with its own crews and materials, and pricing moves with the market. We coordinate it and fold it into your proposal.
- **Retaining walls, grading, and haul-off** — on a sloped lot this can be a significant line item
- **Drainage structures** — French drains, channel drains, and tie-ins beyond standard surface slope
- **In-ground goal systems** — depends on the unit you pick; backboard size, glass vs. acrylic, and adjustable vs. fixed all move the number
- **Lighting** — requires a licensed electrician, and depends on your panel capacity and the run distance from the house
- **Patios, seat walls, and shade structures** at the sideline

**What moves the concrete number most is how much dirt has to move.** A flat, open, accessible backyard is a very different job from a sloped lot that needs a retaining wall and a bobcat threaded between two houses. Access, grade, and drainage are the three variables we look at first, and they're exactly why we won't quote a court sight unseen.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How long does it take to build a backyard basketball court?**
Most courts are 3 to 5 working days on site — excavation and base prep, forming and reinforcement, pour and finish. Add about 7 days before light use and 28 days before heavy use, striping, or an acrylic coating.

**Can I play on it right away?**
Concrete reaches most of its strength in 28 days. You can shoot on it lightly after about a week. Hold off on anything heavy — vehicles, dunking, equipment — for the full 28.

**Do I need a permit for a basketball court in Knox County?**
A ground-level slab usually doesn't require a building permit, but HOA approval, setback requirements, and easements absolutely apply, and rules differ between Knoxville city, Knox County, and the surrounding counties. We check this as part of the consultation.

**Will the court raise my home's value?**
A well-built, well-placed court is a genuine selling feature in family-heavy neighborhoods like Farragut, Hardin Valley, Rocky Hill, and Fountain City. It's also flexible — a buyer who doesn't play basketball still sees a patio, a pickleball court, or a parking pad.

**What's the best direction to face a home court?**
North-south, if the lot allows it. Facing the goal east or west means somebody is shooting into the sun every evening. It's a small detail that makes a real difference in how often the court gets used.

**Can you build a court on a sloped lot?**
Yes — that's most of East Tennessee. It usually means cut-and-fill, sometimes a [retaining wall on the high side](/retaining-walls/), and a drainage plan. We build retaining walls in-house, so it's one crew and one contract instead of coordinating two.

**Can you put pickleball lines on it too?**
Yes, and we recommend it. A pickleball court is 20' x 44', which fits inside most half courts. Two line colors, one surface, twice the use.
